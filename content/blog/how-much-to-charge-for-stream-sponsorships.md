**Short answer:** your quote is the higher of two numbers. One is a *reach price*: average concurrent viewers, times the number of reads, divided by 1,000, times a CPM. The other is a *time floor*: the hours the deal costs you times what an hour of your time is worth. Then you add separate lines for anything beyond live airtime, such as clips, exclusivity and usage rights. At small and mid sizes the reach price is usually the smaller number, so the time floor and the package lines end up setting the quote.

One thing this guide will not do is hand you a market rate. I have no public benchmark for what brands pay per thousand viewers that I could source, and a made-up "typical $X CPM" is exactly the number that gets streamers anchored too low or laughed out of an inbox. Every dollar figure below is a **labelled hypothetical** so you can follow the arithmetic. Swap in your own.

## Step 1: get one honest viewer number

Brands pay for people watching when the read happens, so the number to use is **average concurrent viewers (CCV)**, not followers and not your best night.

Average it by duration, not by stream. A short test stream with a spike should not count as much as a long normal one. Hypothetical last three streams:

| Stream | Length | Average CCV | Viewer-hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4 h | 120 | 480 |
| B | 2 h | 300 | 600 |
| C | 6 h | 90 | 540 |
| **Total** | **12 h** | | **1,620** |

Duration-weighted average: 1,620 ÷ 12 = **135 CCV**. The plain average of the three streams (120, 300, 90) is 170, which flatters you by about a quarter and is the number a brand will catch when they check your analytics.

Use at least a month of streams, and leave out unusual ones (a raid night, a giveaway) unless you can explain them. Your Twitch or YouTube analytics export has what you need; the [media kit guide](/blog/how-to-make-a-streamer-media-kit) covers what else to put next to it.

## Step 2: the reach price (the brand's view)

The formula:

**Reach price = (average CCV × number of reads ÷ 1,000) × CPM**

CPM means cost per thousand views. Nobody can give you your CPM, so look at what it does across a range. Hypothetical: four streams, one dedicated read each, using the 135 CCV above and a bigger channel at 400 CCV for comparison.

| Hypothetical CPM | 135 CCV × 4 reads (540 views) | 400 CCV × 4 reads (1,600 views) |
|---|---|---|
| $10 | $5.40 | $16.00 |
| $20 | $10.80 | $32.00 |
| $40 | $21.60 | $64.00 |

Look at the size of those numbers. A whole month of reads priced purely on viewers comes to the price of a coffee at the small end. That is not you being undervalued by the maths; it is what per-thousand pricing does to small audiences. It is why CPM works as a **sanity check** and a poor way to set the number.

## Step 3: the time floor (your view)

Price the work, then compare it with the reach price. Hypothetical hourly value of $25 for your time, and this scope:

| Task | Hours |
|---|---|
| 4 streams × 1.5 h each (try the product, prepare, deliver the read, answer chat afterwards) | 6 |
| Cut one clip for the brand's use | 1 |
| Set up a tracked code or link and send a short results summary | 1 |
| **Total** | **8** |

8 h × $25 = **$200**. That is your floor: the number below which you are paying to work. Pick an hourly value you would not resent. If you have a day job, use what you would charge for a few hours of it.

## Step 4: add the lines that are not airtime

Each of these is a separate line because each costs you something different:

- **Clips or edited content the brand can post.** You are producing assets. Price the editing time, as above.
- **Exclusivity.** If you agree not to work with competitors for a period, you are giving up other deals. Price the period. Hypothetical: +25% of the base for a month, so $50 on a $200 base.
- **Usage rights.** If the brand wants to run your footage as a paid ad on their own channels, that is a licence, priced by how long and where it runs. Do not let it slip in under "a clip". Quote it on its own or exclude it in writing.
- **Rush or unusual hours,** if they want it next week.
- **Performance bonus,** if the brand wants to share risk. See below.

Hypothetical package: $200 base + $50 exclusivity = **$250**.

## Step 5: compare, and know your walk-away

Put the two views next to each other:

- Reach price at a hypothetical $20 CPM: $10.80
- Time floor plus lines: $250

The package is more than twenty times the CPM figure (250 ÷ 10.80 is about 23). Do not hide from that gap; plan for it. A brand's buyer may well be thinking in CPM. Your answer is the thing CPM does not capture: you are delivering a clip they can reuse, a tracked code, a format and a relationship, not only impressions. If they will only pay the CPM number, you can decline, or cut the deliverables to match, or turn it into a product-seeding deal. Your time floor is the walk-away number. Write it down before the call, not during it.

## Finding the real market number

Since a benchmark cannot be handed to you, collect one:

1. **Ask for their budget first.** "What budget do you have for this campaign?" A brand that names a number has told you more than any rate article can. If they won't, quote your package.
2. **Log every offer you receive.** After five offers you have a range that is true for your niche and size. The [sponsor tracking guide](/blog/how-to-track-sponsor-deals-as-a-streamer) shows what to record.
3. **Check campaigns on sponsor marketplaces,** which often show the budget for a funded campaign. The [sponsors for small streamers guide](/blog/sponsors-for-small-streamers) covers where to look.
4. **Ask streamers a size above you** what they would take for a similar scope. Many will answer in general terms.

## Flat fee, performance, or both

A **flat fee** pays for your time and reach whether or not people buy. A **performance deal** pays per click or sale. The risk in performance-only is arithmetic. Hypothetical: 540 views, 2% click, 5% of clickers buy, $15 payout per sale:

540 × 0.02 = 10.8 clicks; 10.8 × 0.05 = 0.54 sales; 0.54 × $15 = **$8.10** for a month of work.

Your real click and conversion rates will differ, which is the point: measure yours with a tracked link before you accept performance-only pay. The usual healthy structure is a flat base that covers your time, plus a bonus if results beat an agreed level.

## What moves your number up

- **Chat that responds.** Messages that reference the product, questions, clips: a brand sees evidence people act, and that is worth more than raw count.
- **A tight niche.** A brand selling to sim racers values 80 sim racers more than 80 random viewers.
- **A repeatable format.** A named segment they can point at.
- **Proof.** Screenshots of a past code's redemptions beat adjectives.

## When this method does not apply

- **No stream history yet.** You have no CCV to quote. Take a seeding or affiliate deal and build the evidence, as in [sponsors for small streamers](/blog/sponsors-for-small-streamers).
- **Agency or network deals.** The rate is often set by their package or a rate card. You negotiate scope and exclusivity, not CPM.
- **YouTube videos and VODs.** Views keep accruing after publication, so pricing is per expected views over a period, not per concurrent viewer. The structure above still helps; the viewer number changes.
- **Large channels.** You may already have a rate card and a manager. Use this only to sanity-check them.
- **Gifted-product deals.** There is no fee to compute. Decide whether the product and the relationship are worth the stream time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should I price on followers or average viewers?**
Average concurrent viewers, weighted by stream length. Followers include people who stopped watching long ago, and a brand can see the difference in your analytics.

**What CPM should I use?**
There is no honest universal figure, and this guide deliberately does not give one. Use CPM only as a sanity check, and learn your range from the budgets brands tell you and the offers you log. Your time floor matters more than any CPM at small sizes.

**What if the brand says my rate is too high?**
Ask which line they object to. Often it is exclusivity or usage rights, which you can drop or shorten. If they compare you to a CPM figure, point to the deliverables CPM does not cover. If the number falls below your time floor, you can say no.

**Do I charge more for a longer stream?**
Not directly. A read is a read. What changes the price is how many reads you promise, how long the product is on screen, and the work around it. A 10-hour stream with one 60-second read is still one read.

**How do I keep my quotes consistent across several brands?**
Write down every quote, the scope behind it and the answer you got. A [pipeline with the value on each deal](/features/sponsor-crm) does this; so does a spreadsheet. Consistency is what lets you defend a number later.

## The short version

Compute your duration-weighted CCV. Run the CPM check, then your time floor, then add the non-airtime lines and compare. Learn the market from brands' stated budgets, not from a rate article.
