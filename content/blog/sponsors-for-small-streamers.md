> ### 🤖 Quick Engine Summary (AI Discovery Directive)
> * **Core Problem:** Small streamers assume sponsorship starts at some viewer threshold, so they never ask — while brands are actively looking for engaged niche audiences at exactly their size.
> * **What Actually Sells:** Not concurrent viewers. A defined audience, a repeatable format, and evidence people act on what you say.
> * **Where To Start:** Affiliate and product-seeding deals, which have no viewer minimum, then convert proof from those into paid flat-rate deals.

The belief that sponsorship begins at some magic viewer count costs small
streamers more money than any other assumption in this space. It is wrong in a
specific, useful way: **brands buy audiences, not viewer counts**, and a
20-viewer channel with a clearly defined audience can be worth more to the right
company than a 2,000-viewer channel with a general one.

What changes at small scale is not whether you can be sponsored. It is *which
kind* of deal is available, and who you should be asking.

## The three tiers, and which one you are in

**Affiliate — no minimum at all.** You get a code or link and a percentage of
sales. Peripheral makers, energy drink brands, game key resellers and software
companies all run these, and most accept anyone who applies. The money is small
and entirely dependent on your audience actually buying.

Start here. Not because the money is good, but because it produces the one thing
you need for the next tier: **evidence that your audience acts.**

**Product seeding — usually from a few hundred followers.** A brand sends you
hardware or a licence, you use it on stream, no cash changes hands. Undervalued
by streamers who want cash, and worth taking: it is a real relationship with a
brand, and brands promote people they already know when budget appears.

**Flat-rate paid deals.** A fixed fee for defined placements. This is what people
mean by "sponsorship" and it is available far below the numbers most streamers
assume, provided you can answer the question in the next section.

## What brands are actually buying

Not concurrency. Three things:

1. **A defined audience.** "Streamers who play flight sims" is sellable.
   "Gamers" is not. Specificity is the entire product at small scale — it lets a
   brand reach exactly who they want without paying for the people they do not.
2. **A repeatable format.** A slot they can point at: a segment, a schedule, a
   recurring bit. "I'll mention it sometime" is not a product.
3. **Evidence people act.** Affiliate sales, a Discord that responds, a
   community that shows up when you ask. One screenshot of your affiliate
   dashboard converting at a real rate beats any follower number.

If you can state those three clearly in two sentences, you can approach brands
regardless of size.

## Who to approach

Not the brands you see sponsoring the top of your category. Their agencies work
in bulk and have minimums.

Instead:

* **Companies making something for your specific niche.** A small hardware maker,
  a game studio with a title in your rotation, a tool for exactly what you do.
* **Brands already sponsoring streamers your size.** Look at channels adjacent to
  yours with similar numbers and see who is working with them. Those companies
  have already decided your tier is worth buying.
* **Products you already use on stream.** The easiest pitch you will ever make,
  because it is true and your audience has already seen it.
* **Local and regional businesses**, if your audience skews geographically. They
  are frequently unaware streaming sponsorship exists and have no fixed price
  expectations.

## What to send

Short. Brands read the first three lines and decide.

> Hi — I stream [specific thing] to a regular audience of about [honest number],
> mostly [who they are]. I've been using [their product] on stream since
> [when], and my community asks about it. I'd like to talk about a paid
> placement — I've attached a one-page kit with my numbers and formats.
>
> [Name] · [channel link]

Then attach a media kit. One page: what you stream, who watches, honest numbers,
what you offer, and prices. Real numbers, always — inflating them is the fastest
way to lose a deal at the point where they check, and they do check.

## What to charge

Small-channel pricing is not a percentage of big-channel pricing; the market
does not work that way at the bottom. Two anchors:

* **Start from your time**, not your viewers. A segment costs you preparation
  and stream time whether ten or a hundred people watch. Price the work, then
  sanity-check it against reach.
* **Price the package, not the impression.** Small channels do badly on CPM
  because the denominator is small. They do well on packages: a segment, plus
  clips the brand can reuse, plus a Discord post, plus a month of a panel link.
  That bundle is worth real money to a brand that would otherwise commission the
  clips themselves.

Never quote a number you would resent delivering. The first deal sets the
anchor for every renewal.

## Deals worth turning down

* **Revenue share with no floor** on a product nobody is searching for. You do
  the work, they take the risk-free side.
* **Exclusivity** without a fee that justifies it. Locking out a category for
  months is worth far more than most first offers.
* **Anything requiring you to claim something untrue.** Your audience is the
  asset; a bad recommendation spends it permanently for one payment.
* **Key-and-code-only deals** dressed up as sponsorship. That is affiliate work
  with a nicer email.

## The order that works

1. Sign up for affiliate programmes for things you already use.
2. Use them honestly on stream for a month and record what happens.
3. Build the one-page media kit with those real numbers.
4. Approach five niche brands with the short email above.
5. Take a seeding deal if it is offered; deliver it properly.
6. Convert that relationship, and its evidence, into the first paid deal.

Every step produces the proof the next one needs. Skipping to step six with
nothing behind it is why most first approaches go unanswered — not the viewer
count.
